# Measure the AI, don't just ship it

The strongest exam project here is a small web app where a SAM-family model (SAM 2.1-tiny or MobileSAM) turns clicks into masks and a small detector (a YOLO model) proposes boxes, and the app logs every second, click and edit so it can **prove** how much effort the AI saves. Click-to-segment is now standard. CVAT, Label Studio, Roboflow, V7, Encord and X-AnyLabeling all ship it, and text-prompt labeling with SAM 3 or Grounding DINO is where the field is heading. What no vendor publishes is a measured speed-up with a stated method: V7 claims "up to 10x" and AWS claims "up to 70%" lower cost, but neither gives a method. The best public evidence is still Meta's SAM paper, where assisted labeling cut **time per mask from 34 s to 14 s**. So the exam's "measure quality and effort saved" requirement is not a box to tick. It is the gap between this project and the commercial tools. Industry leaders (Apple, Samsung, Google, Adobe, Meta, Tesla, Waymo, MONAI) share five patterns: click or tap prompts, AI proposals verified by humans, confidence gating, a split between client and server compute, and retraining on corrected labels. A student can build all five at small scale. The planned Rust stack (axum plus ONNX Runtime via `ort`) is technically sound and has working Rust examples for SAM and YOLO. To meet the "web application" requirement, the app must be served by axum and used in a normal browser. Tauri v2 then becomes an optional desktop package of the same frontend, not the main deliverable. For the German job market, the project fits the shift to "AI-first, human-verified" labeling and the ONNX/edge deployment skills that German machine-vision employers ask for. Rust is a niche advantage there, not a replacement for Python.

## Click-to-segment is table stakes; measured effort is the open gap

The tools split into open-source leaders (CVAT, Label Studio, plus the lightweight desktop X-AnyLabeling and the browser-only makesense.ai) and commercial platforms, most of which hide prices behind sales calls. Roboflow is a clear exception: its free plan includes 10 credits/month, Core starts at $39/month, and all AI labeling "uses credits" ([Roboflow pricing](https://roboflow.com/pricing)). CVAT Online Solo costs $23–33/month ([CVAT pricing](https://cvat.ai/pricing)). Google left the market entirely: its Vertex AI Data Labeling Service was shut down in 2024 ([Vertex AI deprecations](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/docs/deprecations)). Being free is therefore not a differentiator, because CVAT Community and Label Studio Community already fill that niche.

The AI features have converged. CVAT runs SAM 2 image segmentation with optional box prompts and a SAM 2 tracker for video. Since 2025 its "AI Agents" let users plug in their own YOLO or SAM models ([CVAT Dec 2025 digest](https://www.cvat.ai/resources/changelog/digest-dec-2025)). Label Studio connects SAM 2 through a separate ML-backend server on port 9090, which the user must start and wire up by hand ([Label Studio SAM 2 tutorial](https://labelstud.io/tutorials/segment_anything_2_image)). Roboflow offers Label Assist, Smart Polygon, Box Prompting and batch Auto Label with SAM 3 or Gemini ([Roboflow blog](https://blog.roboflow.com/best-image-annotation-tools/)). V7's changelog of February 2026 adds SAM 3 text-prompt segmentation, and Encord integrates SAM 2 and SAM 3 ([Lightly blog 2026](https://lightly.ai/blog/8-best-cvat-alternatives-for-computer-vision-teams-in-2026)). X-AnyLabeling bundles Grounding DINO, YOLO-World, YOLOE and SAM 3 in a desktop app ([GitHub X-AnyLabeling](https://github.com/CVHub520/X-AnyLabeling)). For export, **COCO JSON, YOLO txt and Pascal VOC XML** are the universal trio ([makesense.ai README](https://github.com/amai-gmbh/make-sense)).

Users complain about the same things across tools. SuperAnnotate's top G2 complaints are performance and a hard learning curve ([G2](https://www.g2.com/compare/labelbox-vs-superannotate)). Roboflow users say credit use "is not always clear upfront" ([G2 Roboflow reviews](https://www.g2.com/products/roboflow/reviews)). Label Studio's XML config and self-hosting take effort, and CVAT lacks curation and training loops ([Lightly blog 2026](https://lightly.ai/blog/8-best-cvat-alternatives-for-computer-vision-teams-in-2026)). CVAT also has no labeling history view ([Roboflow compare](https://roboflow.com/compare-labeling-tools/cvat-vs-label-studio)).

Speed claims are where the market is weakest. V7's "up to 10x" comes with no method ([V7](https://www.v7labs.com/compare/cvat-vs-v7)). AWS's "up to 70%" cost reduction dates from its 2018 launch and has no independent check ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/amazon-sagemaker-ground-truth-build-highly-accurate-datasets-and-reduce-labeling-costs-by-up-to-70)). There is also counter-evidence: Supervisely found **SAM 2 gave almost no time savings** on tomato phenotyping because its boundaries were imprecise ([Supervisely technical report](https://docs.supervisely.com/labeling/overview/live-training/technical-report)). The project's position follows from this. Match the baseline (click-to-segment, detector pre-labels, COCO/YOLO export). Then differentiate on three things: the model works with zero configuration, compute is local and transparent, and a built-in effort meter compares assisted and manual labeling with a stated method.

## Five industry patterns a student can copy at small scale

The big players reveal more design than models. **Apple** published the most open account. Its subject-lifting network resamples the input to 512×512, uses an EfficientNet v2-variant encoder, runs in **under 10 ms** on the iPhone 14 Neural Engine, and shows a mask only when a separate confidence branch scores high enough. Apple also found that mean IoU, precision and recall were not enough to judge product quality, so it added crowd-sourced human ratings ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/salient-object-segmentation)). **Samsung** puts tap or draw-around selection in Gallery ([Samsung support](https://www.samsung.com/sg/support/mobile-devices/how-to-use-ai-select-on-your-galaxy-phone-and-tablet/)). Object Eraser works offline, while Generative Edit needs the cloud ([Samsung SG guide](https://www.samsung.com/sg/mobile/mobile-phone-buying-guide/galaxy-ai-photo-video-editing-guide/)), where Google's Imagen 2 runs on Vertex AI ([Samsung Mobile Press](https://www.samsungmobilepress.com/press-releases/samsung-and-google-cloud-join-forces-to-bring-generative-ai-to-samsung-galaxy-s24-series/)). Samsung has never named its on-device segmentation model. A review notes that the selection misses shadows and attached regions, which is a reminder that humans still need to correct the output ([SamMobile](https://www.sammobile.com/opinion/galaxy-s24-ai-generative-edit-is-brilliant-faulty-intuitive-lacking-all-at-once/)). **Google** splits work the same way: Magic Eraser runs on-device and Magic Editor on servers ([SlashGear](https://www.slashgear.com/1441334/how-to-use-google-ai-magic-editor-pixel-8)). Circle to Search turns a circle, tap or scribble into a selection ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/17/google-introduces-circle-to-search-a-new-way-to-search-from-anywhere-on-android-using-gestures)). **Adobe** shows the Device/Cloud choice directly to the user for Select Subject ([MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/photoshop-adobe-cloud-vs-device-select-subject/)).

Meta's SAM is the closest precedent for this project, because SAM was built *by* an annotation tool. In stage 1 of the data engine, annotators clicked foreground and background points in a browser tool and refined masks with a brush. Time per mask fell from **34 s to 14 s**, which the paper calls 6.5× faster than COCO mask annotation, and the model was retrained six times. In stage 2, confident masks were pre-filled and time rose back to 34 s, because the objects left over were harder. A quality check of 500 professionally corrected images found **94% of auto masks above 0.9 IoU** against the corrections. With the image embedding precomputed, the decoder runs in about **50 ms in a browser on CPU** ([SAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.02643)). SAM 2 extended this to video and reports 37.8 s per frame falling to 4.5 s (8.4×) ([SAM 2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00714)).

At scale the loop is the same: a model pre-labels, humans review (routed by confidence), and the corrections retrain the model. Scale AI routes driving pre-labels to reviewers by model confidence ([Scale (mirror)](https://website-5vy49o7dc-scaleai.vercel.app/self-driving-cars)). Waymo's offboard 3D auto-labels matched human labels in a label study ([Waymo research](https://waymo.com/research/offboard-3d-object-detection-from-point-cloud-sequences/)). Tesla's figures (a team of about 1,000 labelers and semi-auto labeling of fleet clips) come only from secondary coverage of AI Day 2021 ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/tesla-ai-day-2021-review-part-2-training-data-how-does-a-car-learn-e8863ba3f5b0)). **MONAI Label** is the clearest open-source analogue. DeepGrow does click-based segmentation, DeepEdit combines auto-segmentation with click correction, and two active-learning strategies pick the next image to label. In its left-atrium experiment, training could start after 1–2 labeled images ([arXiv 2203.12362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.12362)).

The five shared patterns are click or tap prompts, AI proposal plus human verification, confidence gating, a client/server compute split, and iterative retraining. The first four belong in the core app. Retraining is a stretch goal. Fleet-scale auto-labeling, LiDAR, and Apple-scale crowd evaluation belong in the report only as motivation.

Check every license against the repo's LICENSE file before citing it.

SAM 3 is the obvious temptation and the wrong default. Its text-prompt "label all cars" is what V7 and Roboflow now sell. But it has 848M parameters, gated checkpoints, a custom license, and needs a CUDA GPU, and Meta has published no official ONNX export (only a community INT8 build exists). That clashes with a CPU-friendly Rust and ONNX design. The **Apache 2.0 SAM 2.1-tiny or MobileSAM**, paired with a YOLO detector, covers both assist modes the exam needs: interactive (click to mask) and pre-annotation (detector boxes the user accepts or edits). They can also be chained, so a detector box becomes a SAM box prompt. That mirrors the box-then-correct workflow of Benenson et al. ([CVPR 2019](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2019/html/Benenson_Large-Scale_Interactive_Object_Segmentation_With_Human_Annotators_CVPR_2019_paper.html)). Ultralytics' AGPL-3.0 license is fine for an open-source university repo, but the report should say so explicitly ([Roboflow YOLO26 blog](https://blog.roboflow.com/yolo26/)).

The Rust path has working examples. The `usls` crate, built on `ort`, ships SAM, SAM 2, MobileSAM, EdgeSAM, SAM-HQ, FastSAM and YOLO examples ([docs.rs usls](https://docs.rs/crate/usls/latest/source/src/models/sam/README.md)). Ultralytics keeps an official YOLOv8 ONNX Runtime Rust example ([Ultralytics example](https://dagshub.com/Ultralytics/ultralytics/src/benchmark-format-args/examples/YOLOv8-ONNXRuntime-Rust/README.md)). The browser path is proven too. ONNX Runtime Web with WebGPU sped up the SAM encoder about **19×** and the decoder about **3.8×** over WASM in Microsoft's demo ([Microsoft Open Source blog](https://opensource.microsoft.com/blog/2024/02/29/onnx-runtime-web-unleashes-generative-ai-in-the-browser-using-webgpu/)). SAM's WASM demo uses SharedArrayBuffer, so the server must send COOP/COEP cross-origin isolation headers ([SAM repo docs](https://docsearch.algolia.com/mcp/docs/repo/facebookresearch/segment-anything)). The architecture follows from these facts. Run the heavy image encoder **once per image** in axum with `ort` and cache the embedding. Run the light prompt decoder per click, either on the server or in the browser via onnxruntime-web. Log both latencies, because they are cheap system metrics for the report. One caution on the no-forking rule: `usls` and `ort` are inference libraries, not annotation tools, so using them is allowed. The annotation UI, data model, and export code must be your own, and every library and model must be cited.

## A two-part protocol turns "effort saved" into defensible numbers

The literature measures assistance in two ways, and the project should do both. **Part A, offline model quality, needs no humans.** Use the RITM click simulator: the first click goes at the object's interior center (the maximum of the distance transform), and each next click goes at the center of the largest error region, up to 20 clicks. Report **NoC@85 and NoC@90** (the number of clicks needed to reach 0.85 or 0.90 IoU), mIoU after 1, 3 and 5 clicks, and how many objects fail to reach the threshold within 20 clicks ([RITM arXiv 2102.06583](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.06583); [SAM paper §7.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.02643)). The standard small benchmarks are small enough for one student: GrabCut (50 images), Berkeley (100 masks on 96 images) and DAVIS (345 frames) ([RITM](https://arxiv.org/html/2102.06583)). For detector pre-labels, report COCO mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95 on a val2017 subset ([COCO eval](https://cocodataset.org/#detection-eval)), plus precision and recall at IoU 0.5. Be clear that COCO is *not* unseen data for a COCO-trained YOLO model.

**Part B is a small within-subject timed study.** Use 5–8 participants and about 20 images each with 2–5 objects per image. Each participant labels half the images manually (polygon or box) and half with AI assistance, with order and image sets counterbalanced. The app logs time per object, clicks, vertices, and whether each AI proposal was accepted, edited or deleted, plus the IoU of each final label against ground truth. Report the median and IQR of seconds per object, the speed-up ratio, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Wilcoxon, SUS and NASA-TLX are standard HCI practice and are not taken from a specific annotation paper. Compare the results with published anchors: SAM's 34 s to 14 s per mask, extreme clicking at about 7 s per box versus **34.5 s** for drawn boxes ([Papadopoulos et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02750)), Fluid Annotation at **3× less time than LabelMe** ([arXiv 1806.07527](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07527v5)), and Benenson's masks made **3× faster than polygons at comparable quality** ([CVPR 2019](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2019/html/Benenson_Large-Scale_Interactive_Object_Segmentation_With_Human_Annotators_CVPR_2019_paper.html)). Polygon-RNN shows the click-counting logic: **4.7× fewer clicks** at 78.4% IoU agreement ([arXiv 1704.05548](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1704.05548)).

Two rules from the literature protect against easy criticism. First, compare time only at matched quality, because a faster but worse label is not a saving. That is why both Polygon-RNN and Benenson report IoU agreement alongside speed. Second, report results per object or per class, not only as one average, because SAM's stage 2 showed that gains disappear on hard objects. No standard definition of "correction rate" exists, so define accept, edit, delete and add rates explicitly in the report. Threats to validity to state: learning effects, small samples, overlap between the evaluation data and the models' training data, hardware-dependent latency, and the student acting as both builder and participant.

## Rust is a sound backend, but the browser must be the product

The exam asks for a web application, so the clean reading is a server that serves a browser UI over HTTP. An **axum backend that serves the frontend and a JSON API, opened in Chrome or Firefox, satisfies that requirement without debate**. Tauri v2 wraps the same HTML/TypeScript frontend in a desktop shell. Shipped alone, it invites the objection that the deliverable is a desktop app. The fix is to keep the frontend talking to axum over plain HTTP (not through Tauri-only IPC), make the hosted browser version the main deliverable and demo, and present Tauri as an optional "offline, local-first" package built from the same code. This also mirrors Adobe's Device/Cloud choice and Samsung's split between on-device and cloud work, which gives the report a real precedent for the design.

The Rust choice needs a stated reason, because Python dominates ML. Lightcast counts Python in 258,674 US AI postings in 2025 ([Lightcast](https://lightcast.io/resources/blog/stanford-ai-2026)). The defensible reasons are a single small binary with no Python runtime, predictable inference latency on CPU through ONNX Runtime, memory safety for a server that handles uploaded files, and a direct match to German machine-vision runtime roles (next section). The report should still keep Python where Python is better: exporting models to ONNX, running pycocotools for mAP, and the statistics notebook. Using the mainstream evaluation tooling makes the numbers easier to trust. One practical risk: ONNX Runtime Web's WebGPU build has had bundler problems with Vite ([webgpu-sam2 write-up](https://11ty-site-b0m.pages.dev/software/webgpu-sam2)). Running inference on the server in `ort` avoids that risk, and in-browser decoding can come later.

## Concrete recommendations

**Feature set, ranked by exam value per unit of effort.** The ranking is a judgment based on the exam requirements and the competitor gaps above, not on user research.

| Priority | Feature | Why (exam requirement / precedent) |
|---|---|---|
| Must | Image upload, project/class list, manual box + polygon tools, undo/redo | Upload and label; manual baseline for the study |
| Must | Click-to-segment (positive/negative points, box prompt) with SAM 2.1-tiny or MobileSAM; encoder cached per image | Model assists during annotation; SAM/Samsung/Apple tap pattern |
| Must | Detector pre-labels (YOLO) with a confidence slider: high-confidence shown as proposals, low-confidence flagged | Apple confidence gate, Scale confidence routing, Ground Truth auto-accept |
| Must | Persistence (e.g. SQLite) and export to COCO JSON (polygons/RLE) + YOLO txt; VOC optional | Saved and exportable; universal formats |
| Must | Effort logger and Manual/AI mode toggle: time, clicks, vertices per object; accept/edit/delete/add per proposal | Measure effort saved; no competitor exposes this per session |
| Should | Provenance per annotation (AI-proposed, edited, manual) and history | Fills CVAT's missing history view; feeds quality analysis |
| Should | "Evaluation report" export: mIoU/mAP vs ground truth, speed-up, latencies | Turns the app into its own measuring instrument |
| Stretch | Text-prompt boxes (Grounding DINO or YOLO-World) | Matches the 2025–26 frontier |
| Stretch | One retraining round: fine-tune the detector on corrected labels, show the accept rate rising | Mini SAM data engine / MONAI Label loop |
| Avoid | Video tracking, multi-user QA/consensus, LiDAR, SAM 3 on CPU | High effort, low exam value, or infeasible |

**Evaluation protocol checklist.** Part A (offline): run MobileSAM, SAM 2.1-tiny and SAM 2.1-large through the RITM simulator on GrabCut, Berkeley and DAVIS-345. Report mIoU@1/3/5, NoC@85/90, failures at 20 clicks, and encoder/decoder latency on your own hardware. Run YOLO on a 200–500-image val2017 subset and report mAP@0.5, mAP@0.5:0.95 and precision/recall at IoU 0.5, computed with pycocotools. Part B (user study): 5–8 participants, about 20 images each, within-subject and counterbalanced, with time and clicks logged automatically. Report the median/IQR of seconds per object, the speed-up ratio, a Wilcoxon test, the final IoU against ground truth for both conditions, and results per class. Include at least one failure case (small objects, thin structures, domain shift).

**Report justification points.** Justify the feature set by mapping each must-have to an exam requirement and to an industry precedent (SAM data engine, Apple confidence gating, Scale routing). Justify the models on license (Apache 2.0 SAM 2.1; AGPL YOLO acknowledged), size and CPU latency, and say explicitly why SAM 3 was rejected (848M parameters, gated, needs a GPU, no official ONNX export). Justify the stack as follows: axum serves a browser app, which satisfies "web application"; `ort` gives Python-free, CPU-predictable inference; the encoder/decoder split follows SAM's own design; Tauri v2 is an optional local-first package of the same frontend; Python is kept for export and evaluation. Cite every pretrained model by paper and repo, and state that no annotation tool was forked or wrapped: libraries such as `ort` and `usls` are inference runtimes only. Finally, set your measured speed-up against the SAM, extreme-clicking and Benenson anchors, and against the vendor claims that publish no method.

## Conclusion

The industry has solved *how* to make a model help annotators. Every serious tool clicks to segment with SAM, and every large pipeline runs pre-label, verify, retrain. What it has not done is show, transparently, *how much* that help is worth: vendors publish upper bounds, and independent tests like Supervisely's sometimes find nothing. That shifts the exam's center of gravity. The CV model is a commodity that you cite and integrate. The original contribution is the instrument: an app that logs its own effort, gates its own proposals by confidence, and reports its own quality against ground truth. A project framed this way scores well on "justify and measure", and it is also the "AI-first, human-verified" workflow that annotation platforms and German machine-vision firms are hiring to build.

The main open risk is not technical but definitional. If the examiners read "web application" strictly, a Tauri-first deliverable could cost marks, while a browser-first axum app with Tauri as a bonus costs nothing. The main open uncertainty is empirical: whether SAM-family assistance helps on the student's chosen image domain as much as on COCO-like data. Measuring that honestly, including where it fails, is worth more for both the grade and the CV than a large speed-up figure.
